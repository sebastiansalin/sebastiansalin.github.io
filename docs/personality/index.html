Off the clock

I am a curious mind with plenty of changing interests. Here are some things I like.

Photography

I started with optics — binoculars, a stargazing pair, a jeweller's loupe — because I wanted to see things properly. The camera came after, once I wanted to keep what I was seeing. Everything on this page is mine.

Writing

I write in my Obsidian journal most days. Putting abstract thoughts into words makes them concrete: I can see what I mean, question it, and come back to it later.

Words written since 2025
500,000+
Words per day, most days
~1,000

The journal catches most of what I think and do. Alongside it sit the longer pieces, the ones that only appear once I am forced to finish the sentence.

I write in Obsidian because a nerd can bend it into whatever shape he wants. Notes link to notes, so ideas find each other the way associations do, rather than sitting in folders nobody opens.

It syncs peer to peer with Syncthing. No cloud provider in the middle, no subscription, and nobody else deciding what happens to a decade of my thinking.

Games

I don't play games just to have fun. I play because it's an endless journey toward improvement and mastery. Factorio taught me more about pipelines than any course did. Elden Ring's first boss, hitless and at level one, mostly to find out whether I could. And years of League is why I could design a recommendation algorithm players would actually trust: I knew exactly which numbers lie.

I now prefer the calm ones at the table, since I use plenty of brain power elsewhere. Social deduction is still the favourite, mostly because it is a licence to read people for an hour.

Factorio is the one that changed how I work. Designing a factory is designing a pipeline, and the habit of asking where the bottleneck actually is came from there rather than from a lecture.

Slay the Spire for the feeling of a run where every decision is load-bearing. Terraria and Minecraft for the years of building things with friends, which is most of what I still enjoy about making anything.

I have both run and played Dungeons & Dragons, and written a pile of custom rules and mechanics of my own. Being Dungeon Master is the best improvisation practice I know: a table of five people will always find the one thing you did not prepare.

Making

My roommate and I print D&D figurines and other small things on his FDM printer, which is how a tabletop hobby quietly turned into a manufacturing one. Painting the miniatures may yet drive me toward actual painting.

I owned a resin printer once too. That thing was a chemical hazard on the scale of Chernobyl, so I sold it, and I have never once missed it.

The paintings above came from deciding our walls were too boring. Some photoshopping, some generative AI, and two thoroughly unearned royal portraits. Everyone who visits now knows exactly who is in charge of the apartment.

Thinking

I've collected plenty of thinking tools over the years. Stoicism for what I can and cannot control. Taoism for finding flow and rest. And a long list of other lenses, maps and heuristics I keep testing against reality. I will happily talk about any of it for longer than is reasonable.

Everyone is running a life philosophy whether they have written it down or not. Talking about them is how I find the holes in mine, which is the entire reason I like the conversation.

  • Only writing and talking are real thinking.
  • When working, work fully. When resting, rest fully.
  • The maps are not the territory — but you still need a map.
  • Everyone is capable of mastery. Mostly it is effort, time, and a good mentor.
  • A good metric is time spent. Spend it on what matters.
  • It is always a good idea to go for a walk.

Living well

I'm interested in what makes a good life, and how to make room for it in an ordinary day. Understanding nutrients, going to the gym, walking, and spending time in nature are part of that. So are sleep, rest, and finding balance in my nervous system. And just as importantly: human connection, play, and having something meaningful to do.

I like helping other people make their lives better, too: sharing useful tools, offering practical help or emotional support, and talking things through. I'll happily share advice when it's welcome. Being there for the people around me gives my own life meaning.

I keep coming back to the things that feel natural to us as humans: moving our bodies, being outdoors, sharing food, and spending unhurried time together. I want a life with room for those things, not just a full calendar.
